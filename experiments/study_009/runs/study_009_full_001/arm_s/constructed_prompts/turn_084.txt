    <assistant_message>1. **Core Theoretical Basis**: The Fiscal Theory of the Price Level (FTPL) posits that the price level is determined by the interaction of the monetary sector and the fiscal sector, specifically by the present discounted value of primary government surpluses relative to the nominal value of public debt. It starts from the government budget constraint, asserting that for the government's balance sheet to be sustainable, the real value of outstanding nominal debt must equal the real value of future primary surpluses. If the nominal debt is fixed in the short run, any shock to fiscal policy (like an unexpected increase in deficits) or the price level must be offset by an adjustment in the price level to maintain this equilibrium.

2. **Distinction from the Quantity Theory of Money**: The traditional Quantity Theory of Money (QTOM), often summarized by the equation $MV=PY$, suggests that the price level is determined primarily by the money supply ($M$) and its velocity ($V$), treating fiscal policy as secondary or neutral. FTPL argues that the money supply is an endogenous variable; the central bank cannot independently set the price level by simply changing $M$. Instead, the price level adjusts to ensure that the real money demand equals the real money supply required to satisfy the government's intertemporal budget constraint.

3. **Implications for Fiscal-Monetary Interaction**: FTPL implies that monetary and fiscal policies are not independent. If a central bank engages in expansionary monetary policy (increasing money supply) but the government does not adjust its fiscal stance to support the higher nominal value of debt (e.g., by lowering taxes or increasing spending), the price level will not rise as predicted by QTOM. Conversely, if fiscal policy becomes unsustainable (e.g., massive debt issuance without credible future surpluses), the price level must rise to reduce the real value of that debt, regardless of the central bank's interest rate decisions. This suggests that fiscal dominance can dictate inflation outcomes.

4. **The Role of Active and Passive Policies**: FTPL introduces a classification of regimes based on whether fiscal or monetary policy is "active" or "passive." In a passive fiscal/active monetary regime, the government adjusts taxes/spending to satisfy the budget constraint for any given price level, allowing the central bank to control inflation via interest rates. In an active fiscal/passive monetary regime (which FTPL suggests is common), the government sets taxes/spending independently, and the price level must adjust to satisfy the budget constraint, effectively making fiscal policy the driver of inflation.
